Banks and payment firms fell the most, because they live on Brazil's own interest rates and spending. Firms that earn in dollars, such as Vale and Embraer, fell the least. Bolsonaro has asked for the result to be cancelled, which most banks expect to fail.

One view is that this is a buying chance: Brazil is a cheap commodity exporter, and its Congress, which leans right, can block big spending. The opposite view is that public debt has already climbed to 83% of the economy, so a 4th Lula term keeps interest rates high for years.

Reading between the lines, Brazil was the emerging market foreign funds liked most, and India the one they liked least. Some hope the crash sends money back to India, which is now cheaper than usual. But funds that are losing money elsewhere usually cut their bets everywhere first, so this is a hope, not a flow anyone has seen yet.

How it all connects

Each force feeds the next one. War keeps oil dear, dear oil weakens the rupee and lifts prices, and that makes the central bank raise rates. Higher rates make bonds pay more and shares look less attractive, and investors who bought small shares with borrowed money are forced to sell.

STEP 1War near Hormuz — attacks on Saudi pipelines keep Brent near $115
STEP 2India pays more — its crude basket costs about $120 and the rupee is above 95 per dollar
STEP 3Rates rise — repo at 5.50%; the 30-year bond yield at an 8-year high
STEP 4Foreign money leaves — ₹48,056 crore of Indian shares sold in October
STEP 5Small shares fall — loans against shares unwind; the smallcap index is 12% below its high

Reading between the lines, the same chain runs in the US, where AI companies now borrow and sell new shares on a huge scale. That adds to the pile of bonds and shares the world has to buy, which is part of why yields rise everywhere. So the AI story in section 09 is also an Indian interest-rate story.

The price of money: rates rise and lenders get nervous

Central banks, bond yields and the cracks in risky lending

In short
  • The US central bank is expected to hold its rate on 28 October but may raise it in December.
  • India's long-term borrowing cost hit its highest level since 2018 after the RBI raised rates.
  • The riskiest US companies now pay about 18% a year to borrow, a clear sign of stress.

The US Federal Reserve, led by Chair Kevin Warsh, raised its rate in September to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%. Most central banks decide again in the next few days.

Bond markets: too much debt to sell

Governments and AI companies both need to borrow a lot, and lenders want more interest to take it all. The US 10-year yield (the yearly return a lender demands to lend to the US government for 10 years) is close to 5%.

  • France — its 10-year yield rose above the US one for the first time since 2013, to about 5.01%, amid street protests; Goldman Sachs sees the 2027 deficit at 5.4% of output.
  • Japan — Japanese investors sold foreign bonds for 5 weeks in a row, about $22 billion, because bonds at home now pay well.
  • Banks — Dave McKay, head of Royal Bank of Canada, named long-term yields and the supply of debt as the biggest threat to banking over the next 10 years.
  • HSBC — moved government bonds from its lowest setting to above normal, and cut shares from its highest setting to neutral.

Reading between the lines, when Japanese savers bring money home, one of the biggest buyers of US and European bonds steps back just as supply rises. That lifts yields worldwide, and India's yields find it hard to fall while the US pays close to 5%.

Cracks in risky lending

The weakest US companies now pay about 18% a year to borrow, the most since 2020. Stress is also showing in private lending funds and in airline debt.

  • CCC bonds — the riskiest grade yields about 18.0%; the extra over safe bonds is 13.4%, wider than the 2022 peak.
  • Blackstone — its private credit fund BCRED got requests to take out 10% of its value against a 5% limit, and paid about half.
  • JetBlue — its lenders hired restructuring advisers.
  • Goldman Sachs — said its own gloomy credit forecast was not gloomy enough as AI stress reaches bonds.
  • Safer company debt — still calm; a key spread index is 63.5, below its March high of 68.

One view is that this is a slow, orderly widening, one company at a time. The opposite view is that the AI boom has turned from a share-market story into a debt story, and debt problems spread faster than share-price falls.

03

Oil, ships and war: the Gulf holds its breath

Why oil stays dear even when talks begin, and what India pays

In short
  • Houthi rebels hit the Saudi pipeline that carries oil around the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Saudi, Pakistani and Turkish leaders met Iran in Tehran, while the US said it could strike within 96 hours of an order.
  • India pays about $120 a barrel for its crude, the most since 2012.

A pipeline under fire and talks in Tehran

The Houthis, an Iran-backed group in Yemen, fired missiles at Saudi Arabia, aiming at the East-West pipeline and the port of Yanbu. That pipeline is Saudi Arabia's main route around the Strait of Hormuz and carried more than 5 million barrels a day.

  • Damage — pumping stations PS8, PS9 and PS11 and Yanbu were hit about a month earlier and still do not run at the normal rate; the Rabigh refinery caught fire.
  • Tehran meeting — Saudi Defence Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin met Iran's security council; Qatari mediators were also there.
  • Iran's offer — Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi put forward a 7-day plan to reopen Hormuz, with conditions.
  • US — forces could strike within 96 hours of an order, most likely after the 3 November elections; President Trump said the US would not strike "today".
  • Incidents — Iran said it shot down a US drone; a tanker was struck in Hormuz on 23 October; an oil spill off the UAE coast closed beaches.
  • Flights — Air India and Air India Express stopped Riyadh and Dammam flights until 7 November.

One view is that this is the most important step towards peace in 6 months, because Gulf states now talk to Tehran directly. The opposite view is that nothing basic has changed: plans to open Hormuz "for now" in return for payments to Iran settle nothing, and the US is preparing a bigger attack.

Reading between the lines, the Mecca pact (a defence pact of Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan) shows that Gulf states no longer rely only on the US to protect them. Pakistan's army chief is now a go-between with Iran. That matters for India, which buys most of its oil from the Gulf and now sees its rival at the centre of Gulf security talks.

05

India's economy: busy builders, dry fields and choosy shoppers

What growth, festive buying, gold, phones and government projects show

In short
  • Growth looks strong on paper, but households are spending less freely than the headline suggests.
  • Half of India is abnormally dry, which threatens farm incomes and rural loans.
  • Chinese brands now sell their smallest share of India's phones in 8 years.

Strong growth, softer shoppers

Tanay Dalal, chief economist at Axis Bank, raised his growth forecast for FY27 to 7.3% from 7.1%. But he says spending is weaker than it looks, because higher prices, not more buying, drive much of the rise in shop sales.

  • Weak signs — air travel, petrol use and UPI growth have slowed, GST collections have eased, and consumer loans are growing fast.
  • Government help — indirect support since the war is about 1.7% of GDP.
  • Private investment — narrow: mostly power, roads and data centres.
  • Small firms — 75% of manufacturing firms have fewer than 5 workers, according to the IMF.
  • Foreign money — the trade gap India can comfortably fund has shrunk from about 2% of GDP to about zero, because foreign money is leaving.
  • Steel — JSW Steel expects India to use 177 million tonnes in FY27; government building spend rose 18% in the first 5 months.

One view is that India's growth is real, led by government building and strong company sales (section 07). The opposite view is that it leans on public money and price rises, while ordinary households hold back.

Dry fields and rural risk

A weak monsoon and El Niño (a warming of the Pacific Ocean that often brings drought to India) have left 51.2% of India abnormally dry or worse. Lenders are already planning for it.

  • Shriram Finance — cut its loan growth goal for FY27 to 15-17%, naming El Niño.
  • AU Small Finance Bank — tractor loans are already slipping.
  • States to watch — Karnataka and Maharashtra come up most often; a Maharashtra farm-loan waiver could also slow repayments.
  • IndusInd Bank — on "wait and watch" for small loans to rural women's groups.

Power and storage: India runs short after sunset

Why the grid strains in the evening, what a new auction shows, and who is getting orders

In short
  • India's grid fell short by 7.72 GW on the evening of 9 September, after solar power faded.
  • A new auction priced coal-like clean power from solar, wind and batteries at about the cost of new coal.
  • Makers of transformers, cables and towers keep winning large orders.

The evening gap

On 9 September, India's grid was short of 7.72 GW in the evening, and power exchange prices hit their ₹10 ceiling. On 12 October, 22 GW of coal plants tripped without warning.

  • Coal stocks — 77 coal plants were critically short of coal.
  • Bids — buyers sought about 5 times the power on offer.

One view is that this proves India needs more coal: about 47.5 GW of coal plants are being built, and Adani Power alone plans to more than double its capacity. The opposite view is that batteries can now fill the evening gap for less.

An auction that changes the maths

In August, SECI (the government's clean-power buyer) auctioned clean power that must behave like a coal plant, running at least 90% of the time in hours the buyer picks. The winning price was about ₹5.25 a unit.

  • Size — 1,000 MW, 16 bidders and 7 winners; the price is fixed for 25 years.
  • Juniper Green Energy — won the most, 230 MW, using 795 MW of solar, 75 MW of wind and 2,200 MWh of batteries.
  • Penalty — 1.5 times the tariff for power not supplied.
